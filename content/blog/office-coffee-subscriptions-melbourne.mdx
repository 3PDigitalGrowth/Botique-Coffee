## What does "office coffee subscription" usually mean?

An office coffee subscription usually means one of three things: a pod delivery, a bean-only plan, or a machine rental. Pods and beans supply consumables only. A rental supplies the equipment too. The difference matters because the equipment, not the consumable, sets the ceiling on quality and reliability.

### Pod deliveries

Pods are convenient on day one. You get a small machine, a box of capsules and a reorder schedule. For a very small team, that can be enough.

The trouble starts as headcount grows. Pod machines are built for a household, not for a queue of people at 8:45am. Drink variety is limited by whatever the pod system offers, and you are tied to one supplier's capsules. There is also the waste: every cup creates a used capsule.

### Bean-only plans

Bean subscriptions from specialty roasters are often excellent. The beans are fresh and the roast is good. But the plan ends at the delivery door.

You still need a machine that can grind, extract and steam milk consistently. Most offices already own something. It is usually a bean-to-cup unit bought for a household, or a donated machine nobody descales. Café-grade beans through that machine produce a better version of mediocre coffee.

### Machine rentals

A rental puts a commercial machine in your office and keeps it running. Done properly, it includes the install, staff training, regular servicing and a supply of beans and consumables. This is the version that matches what the search term should mean for a workplace, and it is the only one of the three that closes the café gap.

## Why the machine matters more than the beans

The machine matters more than the beans because extraction, milk texture and consistency all depend on the equipment. A commercial machine holds temperature and pressure under repeated use. A domestic unit does not. Beans set the flavour potential, but the machine decides how much of it reaches the cup.

### Commercial versus domestic in a busy office

An office is a peak-demand environment. Coffee is not made evenly through the day. It is made in clusters: arrival, mid-morning, after lunch, before a big meeting.

Commercial machines are designed for that rhythm. They recover quickly between drinks, steam milk without a long wait, and are built to be serviced rather than replaced. Domestic machines are designed for a few drinks a day in a kitchen.

In my experience, a home-grade Jura in an 80-person office will break within a month. That is not a criticism of the Jura. It is the wrong tool for the job.

### Bigger is not better: fit matters

The opposite mistake is just as common. Suppliers upsell. A 12-person team does not need a $15,000 Eversys. It needs a machine sized to 12 people, which is cheaper to rent and simpler to use.

I recommend the machine that fits the team's actual size, even when that is the cheaper option. A rental that is right for your team is worth more to me over five years than an inflated first invoice. We have installed machines across teams of all sizes, up to 400+ people at a single client, and the principle is the same at every scale: match the machine to the people.

### Beans still matter, once the machine is right

Once the equipment is correct, bean selection becomes worth the effort. We use what we call a Curated Coffee Plan. We ask about your team's drink preferences (espresso versus milk-based, strength, any dislikes), start on a well-matched blend, then adjust based on team feedback within the first month. It is how we avoid defaulting to a one-size supply and get to dialled-in beans that suit the people drinking them.

## What is the hidden catch in many office coffee subscriptions?

The hidden catch is the commitment attached to the equipment: fixed terms, exit fees, automatic renewals and support routed through helpdesks. The price looks simple at signing. The cost appears later, when the service is poor or the machine no longer suits your team and leaving is expensive.

### Lock-in terms and exit fees

Equipment rental and lease agreements commonly run for a fixed term. If the machine is wrong for your team, or service drops off, you are still paying until the term ends. Some agreements add early termination fees or renew automatically unless you give notice in a narrow window.

The ACCC publishes guidance on unfair contract terms, and protections can apply to small businesses signing standard form contracts. It is worth reading the guidance before you sign anything with a long term or a heavy exit clause. Read the renewal and termination sections twice.

### Call-centre escalation

The second catch is the support model. Many corporate coffee suppliers route issues through helpdesks, ticket systems and tiered support teams. You describe the problem to someone who has never seen your machine. They log a ticket. Someone else books a technician.

Most faults can be talked through in two minutes when the person answering knows your setup. A slow descale, a grinder setting that drifted, a milk wand that needs cleaning: none of these needs a ticket. Same-day or next-day on-site response is only possible when there is no internal runaround to get through first.

Our policy is no call centres, no corporate runaround. One number, one person.

### What a breakdown costs you

Consider a busy Melbourne workplace where the machine goes down during peak hours. People queue, then give up, then walk out to the café. Meetings start late. Morale takes a small hit each time. The cost is not the repair. It is the disruption. We hear this regularly from clients, including one busy Melbourne workplace that told us regular scheduled servicing and direct personal contact meant the team always had coffee when they needed it most, with no more havoc from a broken machine.

## What should you ask any provider before signing?

Before signing, ask five things: how long the contract runs, what it costs to leave, how fast service responds, who you actually call, and whether the machine is matched to your headcount. The answers show whether you are buying a service or a long-term obligation.

### Contract and exit questions

- What is the minimum term, and does it renew automatically?
- What does it cost to exit early, and what notice is required?
- Who pays for pickup if I leave?
- Can I change machines if my team grows or shrinks?

If the answer to the first question is anything other than "there isn't one", ask why. A provider confident in their service does not need a long term to keep you.

### Service and accountability questions

- What is your typical response time on a service call, and is that across all clients or only a premium tier?
- When something breaks, who answers the phone?
- Do I deal with the same person every time?
- How often is the machine serviced, and is that included?
- Will you tell me if a smaller or cheaper machine suits us better?

The last question is the honesty test. A good provider will sometimes recommend less.

### Supply and setup questions

- Is installation and staff training included?
- Who matches the beans to our team, and can we change them?
- Are beans, milk-related consumables and hot chocolate supplied on a regular schedule?
- Will someone visit the site before recommending a machine?

## Is a rental subscription right for your office?

A month-to-month rental suits most offices that want café-quality coffee without a long commitment. It fits best when your team is large enough to outgrow a domestic machine, when downtime is disruptive, and when you want one accountable contact. It suits fewer offices where coffee is rarely used.

### A quick fit checklist

A rental is likely right for you if most of these are true:

- Your team regularly buys café coffee because the office coffee is not good enough.
- You have a domestic machine, or pods, that struggles at peak times.
- You do not want to be locked into a multi-year term.
- You want installation, training and servicing handled for you.
- You want to deal with one person, not a helpdesk.
- Your team size may change over the next year.
- Coffee and hot chocolate are part of how you keep staff happy.

A different option may suit you if you have a very small team, rarely make coffee, or already own a commercial machine that is well maintained. In that case, a bean supply plan or a servicing arrangement might be enough. I will say so on the phone. If the fit is not right, I would rather point you elsewhere than sign you up.
