## Why Industry Experience Matters More Than a Logo Wall

The difference between a 12-person design studio and a 90-person warehouse isn't just headcount. It's power supply, plumbing access, staff turnover, shift patterns, and how coffee fits into the working day. A machine that suits one will fail or frustrate the other, which is why genuine industry experience matters more than any client list.

A small studio usually has one power point behind a bench, a casual social coffee culture, and low volume but high expectations on quality. A warehouse might run three shifts, push through a far higher daily cup count than an office with the same headcount, and need a machine that survives rough handling and inconsistent cleaning routines. Neither business is wrong about what it wants. They just want completely different things.

This is exactly why the on-site visit matters before any machine gets recommended. In our process, that visit runs about 30 minutes and covers power, plumbing and bench space properly, before anyone talks machine models. Skip that step, and you end up with a beautiful machine that doesn't fit the bench, or doesn't have the water pressure it needs.

A logo wall shows who signed up. It doesn't show who's still happy five years later. Our average client relationship sits north of 5 years, across 200+ active Melbourne workplaces, and that number matters more to me than any single brand name I could put on a page.

## Professional Services and Corporate Offices: Peak Hours and Meeting Room Standards

Corporate offices need a machine that handles a sharp 8am to 10am rush and a second wave after lunch, without a queue forming at the machine. It also needs to look right in a meeting room, because visitors judge a business partly on the coffee it serves them.

CBD and inner-suburb office fitouts are usually tight on bench space, and the coffee point often sits right off the boardroom, so appearance matters as much as output. Volume matters too: a 60-person office isn't making 60 coffees spread evenly across the day, it's making 25 of them in a 45-minute window.

One mid-size Melbourne office I worked with had an existing coffee setup that wasn't meeting the team's needs, and it showed in how staff talked about it. We upgraded them to a WMF commercial machine, with full install, staff training and ongoing service built into the rental from day one. The change was noticeable across the whole office. The site manager told me the team could make good coffee and hot chocolate easily every day, and the quality held for years afterwards, not just in the first month.

For most corporate offices, the right fit is an automatic bean-to-cup machine with dual milk handling, sized for the actual headcount rather than the biggest unit on the price list. ## Medical, Dental and Allied Health: Hygiene, Speed and Simplicity

Medical and dental clinics need a machine that's easy to keep clean between patients, simple enough for rotating reception and clinical staff to operate without training, and fast enough to make a coffee in a short gap between appointments.

Hygiene routines in a clinical setting aren't optional extras, they're part of how the whole practice runs. A machine with auto-rinse cycles and removable, easily sanitised milk components fits that environment far better than a domestic-style unit with hidden parts. The Australian Dental Association's infection control guidance sets a high bar for cleanliness across a practice, and a coffee machine sitting in a staff kitchen isn't exempt from that expectation, even if it's rarely the first thing on a practice manager's mind.

Staff breaks in clinics are short, sometimes five to ten minutes between patients, so one-touch operation matters more here than almost anywhere else. There's no time to fuss over a manual espresso setup, and staff rosters change often enough that anyone using the machine needs to get a decent coffee first try, no barista skill required.

When we onboard a clinic, we run what I call the Curated Coffee Plan: we ask about the team's actual drink preferences, whether that's mostly milk-based flat whites or straight espresso, start them on a well-matched blend, and adjust based on feedback within the first month. It's a small step that avoids handing every clinic the same generic bean regardless of what staff actually drink. ## Warehouses, Trades and Manufacturing: Built for Shift Work and High Throughput

Warehouse and trade environments need a machine built to survive dust, temperature swings and constant use across shift changes, not a delicate benchtop unit designed for a boutique café. Reliability matters more than finesse here, because a broken machine mid-shift causes real disruption.

Shift patterns push cup counts higher than headcount alone would suggest. Early starts, smoko breaks and three-shift operations mean a 40-person warehouse can push through more coffee in a day than a 60-person office running standard hours. The machine needs commercial-grade components that handle that volume without constant intervention.

A busy Melbourne workplace I look after used to lose real time every time their old machine went down mid-shift. Once we put in a properly maintained machine with regular scheduled servicing, and gave the site a direct line to me personally rather than a general enquiries number, that disruption stopped. The site lead told me reliable, regular service meant the team always had coffee when they needed it most, and the breakdowns that used to cause genuine havoc on the floor simply don't happen anymore.

## Education, Community and Not-for-Profit: Budget Certainty Without Lock-In

Schools, community organisations and not-for-profits need predictable monthly costs and the flexibility to change or cancel without penalty, because budgets are often tied to grant cycles or enrolment numbers that shift from year to year.

Budget certainty comes from a fixed monthly rental with no capital outlay, which is far easier for a finance committee or board to approve than a large upfront purchase. That's often the deciding factor for a community organisation weighing up whether coffee for staff and volunteers is even in scope this year.

Month-to-month flexibility is where our model actually differs from most of the market. Every rental we run comes with one month's notice to exit and free machine pickup if the arrangement doesn't work or the funding changes. No lock-in, ever. In an industry where equipment rental usually means a locked-in term to protect the supplier's revenue, that's a deliberate choice on our end, not a gap in our terms and conditions.

Staffroom sharing is common in this category too: one machine servicing a wide mix of teaching staff, admin, and volunteers, all with different preferences and different familiarity with the equipment. Simple, durable machines with straightforward operation work best, the same principle that applies in clinics, just for a different reason.

## Creative, Tech and Hospitality-Adjacent Workplaces: Café-Quality Expectations

Creative studios, tech companies and hospitality-adjacent businesses employ people who buy coffee from a café every day around the corner. Anything less than café-quality in the office gets noticed immediately, so these workplaces need genuine espresso results, not a token gesture machine.

Staff in these industries know good coffee, because they're comparing the office machine directly against the $5 flat white they'd otherwise be buying next door. That comparison is unforgiving. Dialled-in beans, correct dosing and a machine capable of decent milk texture aren't nice-to-haves here, they're the baseline expectation.

A Melbourne business trading in a busy retail strip needed to serve consistently great coffee to staff and customers every single day, with no room for an off day given how visible the coffee was to the public. We supplied fresh beans on a regular delivery cycle and kept the machine fully maintained, managing both personally rather than outsourcing either. ## How to Size and Specify a Machine for Your Workplace

Sizing a machine correctly starts with three numbers: headcount, realistic cups per day, and what power and water access exists at the site. Get those right, and the rest of the decision, which machine, what beans, follows naturally rather than being guesswork.

Headcount and cups per day aren't the same figure. An office of 60 with two work-from-home days a week and a lot of meetings offsite won't drink like an office of 60 that's fully in-house five days a week. Power and water access also matter more than most people expect: a plumbed-in machine needs a water line nearby, and higher-output machines need proper single-phase power, not a shared power board.

We run every new client through what I call the Six-Step Process, designed to take most Melbourne workplaces from first enquiry to an installed, working machine in 5 to 7 business days:

1. **Enquiry** (about 2 minutes): you tell us basic team details.
2. **Phone call with me** (15 to 20 minutes): we shortlist machines and talk rough pricing.
3. **On-site visit** (30 minutes): we check power, plumbing and bench space in person.
4. **Install day** (45 minutes): the machine is connected, the grinder is dialled in, and shots are tested before we leave.
5. **First brew and training** (20 minutes): at least two staff are trained, and a cheat sheet is left on-site.
6. **Ongoing rhythm**: weekly or fortnightly service visits, beans and consumables topped up so your team never goes without coffee.

We've run this process for teams as small as a handful of people and for a single office of 400+ people, our largest install to date. The sizing conversation is identical either way: what does this team actually need, not what's the biggest machine on the list. That's a point of integrity for us, not a sales tactic.
