Good coffee machine installation and staff training means the machine is connected correctly, at least two staff learn basic operation, cleaning and milk handling on install day, a simple checklist stays by the machine, and one named person answers the phone afterwards. That combination delivers café-quality coffee in a Melbourne office from the first week.

Most office managers worry about the same thing before a machine arrives. Not the plumbing, but the people. Will staff waste beans, scorch the milk, leave the drip tray overflowing and then let the whole thing fall into disrepair?

That worry is fair, and it is fixable. This article covers the people side of installation: the handover, the training and the first few weeks of use. It deliberately skips the technical install steps (power, water, drainage and bench space), which sit in our separate office coffee machine installation guide. Here, the focus is on how your team uses the machine once it is on the bench.

## Why does staff training matter as much as the machine?

Because a commercial machine only performs as well as the people using it. Without training, staff guess at settings, skip cleaning, overheat milk and ignore early warning signs. The result is bitter coffee, wasted beans and avoidable breakdowns. Training turns good equipment into a reliable daily habit for the whole office.

We have spent 17 years focused exclusively on Melbourne workplace coffee, and we now look after 200+ active Melbourne workplace clients (our own data, as of 2026). The pattern is consistent. Two offices can rent the identical machine. One produces coffee people queue for. The other produces coffee people complain about. The difference is rarely the hardware.

### What untrained use actually costs

Untrained use shows up in four places:

- **Coffee quality.** Staff change grinder settings to fix a problem that was really a dirty group head, and the dialled-in beans stop tasting right.
- **Waste.** Shots get dumped, milk is over-poured and thrown out, and beans are wasted while people experiment.
- **Breakdowns.** Skipped cleaning and ignored warnings turn minor faults into service calls.
- **Morale.** When the machine is unreliable or the coffee is poor, people drift back to the café down the street. That is lost time and a weaker office culture.

Chris Prokopiou, our founder, has always held that instant coffee in an office never made sense in a city like Melbourne. People here know good coffee. Once you install a proper machine, you owe your team the knowledge to use it properly.

### Training starts with the right machine

Training cannot rescue the wrong machine. A 12-person team does not need a $15,000 Eversys. An 80-person team will break a home-grade Jura within a month. Matching the machine to your real team size is the first step of any handover, because the simplest machine that fits your volume is also the easiest to teach.

## What should an installation-day handover cover?

![Four-panel diagram of coffee machine handover topics](/blog-images/coffee-machine-installation-staff-training-melbourne/img-1.png)

A good handover covers four things: how to make the drinks your team actually orders, how to clean the machine each day, how to handle milk safely, and who to call when something goes wrong. It should be practical, hands-on and done at the machine, not explained from a manual.

This section describes general best practice for any commercial machine handover. Use it as a yardstick when comparing suppliers, including us.

For context, our own Six-Step Process includes a first brew and training step on install, where at least two staff are trained and a cheat sheet is left behind. We keep it short and practical on purpose. The aim is confidence, not a lecture. The process typically takes most Melbourne clients from first call to installed machine in 5 to 7 business days (our own data).

### 1. Basic operation

Staff should leave the handover able to make the drinks your team orders most. That means:

- Making a flat white, latte, cappuccino, long black and hot chocolate if those are on your menu.
- Understanding what the buttons, screen and warning messages mean.
- Knowing how to fill the bean hopper, and which beans go in it.
- Knowing what not to touch, especially grinder settings.

Trainees should make the drinks themselves, not watch someone else do it. People learn with their hands. Ask the trainer to stay until each person has made at least one drink unaided.

### 2. Daily cleaning habits

Cleaning is the single biggest factor in long-term coffee quality and machine life. The handover should show the daily routine on the actual machine, following the manufacturer's instructions. In general, that includes:

- Purging the group heads and wiping the steam wand straight after each use.
- Emptying the drip tray and any used-coffee bin.
- Rinsing jugs and wiping down the bench.
- Running any automatic rinse or cleaning cycle the machine provides.

If a supplier cannot show you the daily routine in a few minutes of demonstration, the routine is too complicated for an office.

### 3. Milk handling

Milk is where most office machines fall down. Milk residue left in a wand or jug goes off, smells and affects taste. A good handover shows:

- How to steam milk without scalding it, and why a hot steam wand needs care to avoid burns.
- Wiping and purging the wand immediately after steaming.
- Using clean jugs, and never topping up old milk with fresh milk.
- Keeping milk refrigerated, and discarding leftover steamed milk rather than re-steaming it.

Food Standards Australia New Zealand (FSANZ) publishes food safety guidance for businesses that handle food and drink, and WorkSafe Victoria publishes general workplace safety guidance for employers. Both are worth a look if your office wants a documented hygiene routine.

### 4. Who to call

The last part of the handover is the simplest and the most overlooked. Staff should know exactly who to ring if something looks wrong. One name, one phone number, written on the cheat sheet and stuck on the machine. If the answer is a helpdesk or a ticket portal, expect delays and lost context.

## How do you build simple daily and weekly habits around the machine?

![Cleaning checklist on cupboard beside an office coffee machine](/blog-images/coffee-machine-installation-staff-training-melbourne/img-2.png)

Build habits around a one-page checklist stuck near the machine. Daily tasks take only a few minutes: purge, wipe, empty, rinse and check supplies. Weekly tasks go slightly deeper. Name an owner for each task, keep the wording simple, and make the checklist easier to follow than to ignore.

A checklist works because it removes the need to remember. It also removes the awkward question of whose job it is. Here is a sample you can adapt. Always defer to your machine's manufacturer instructions and your supplier's guidance.

| Task | How often | Suggested owner |
|---|---|---|
| Purge group heads and wipe steam wand after use | Every use | Whoever made the drink |
| Rinse milk jugs | Every use | Whoever made the drink |
| Empty drip tray and used-coffee bin | Daily | Nominated daily owner |
| Wipe down machine and bench | Daily | Nominated daily owner |
| Run the machine's rinse or cleaning cycle | Daily, end of day | Nominated daily owner |
| Check bean hopper, milk and cup stock | Daily | Nominated daily owner |
| Check water filter indicator and warning messages | Weekly | Office lead |
| Deeper wipe of surrounding area and fridge shelf | Weekly | Office lead |
| Report anything unusual to your supplier | As soon as noticed | Anyone |

### Make the checklist stick

A checklist only helps if people use it. Four rules help:

1. **Keep it to one page.** If it needs two, it is too long.
2. **Rotate the daily owner.** A roster spreads the load and avoids resentment.
3. **Put it where the work happens.** Stick it on the wall or cupboard beside the machine, not in a shared drive.
4. **Name an office lead.** One person owns the weekly tasks and the relationship with the supplier.

## What mistakes do staff make in the first month?

The most common first-month mistakes are skipping daily cleaning, changing grinder settings, leaving milk out, mixing the wrong beans in the hopper and ignoring warning messages. Each is easy to prevent with a clear rule, a visible checklist and one person to ask when someone is unsure.

### Skipping cleaning when the office is busy

Cleaning gets dropped first on busy days. Coffee oils build up, milk residue hardens and flavour suffers within days. **Fix:** make the end-of-day rinse a rostered task, not a goodwill gesture.

### Adjusting the grinder

Someone decides the coffee tastes weak and winds the grinder finer. Now it is too bitter, and three more people have a go. **Fix:** a simple rule that nobody touches the grinder. If the coffee tastes off, call your supplier. We dial in the beans on install day, and a quick phone call is almost always faster than guesswork.

### Mixing beans or overfilling the hopper

Staff sometimes top up the hopper with whatever beans are in the cupboard. Different roasts behave differently, and the grinder settings no longer match. **Fix:** one bean type, one person filling the hopper, and a supplier who keeps supplies topped up so nobody improvises.

### Leaving milk out or topping it up

Milk left on the bench or topped up in the jug causes the smell and sour taste that puts people off office coffee. **Fix:** milk stays in the fridge, jugs get rinsed, and leftover steamed milk is discarded.

### Ignoring warning messages

A low water or maintenance light is an early warning, not a suggestion. Ignoring it is how a small fault becomes a breakdown in peak hours. **Fix:** put the rule on the cheat sheet: any warning message, call the number.

### Assuming the first blend is final

This one is a supplier mistake as much as a staff one. A blend that suits one team does not suit another. Our Curated Coffee Plan starts by asking about your team's drink preferences, such as espresso or milk-based, strength and any dislikes. We start on a well-matched blend, then adjust based on team feedback in the first month. Tell your supplier what your team actually thinks. Silence helps nobody.

## How do you train new starters after the first day?

Treat every new starter to a short induction at the machine, not a favour. Pair them with a trained colleague, walk through the checklist once, and have them make a drink under supervision. A short session saves weeks of bad habits passed along informally.

This is the part most offices forget. Staff turn over, and the people trained on install day move teams or leave. Within a year, many people using the machine have learnt from someone who learnt from someone else, and the original instructions are long gone.

A simple workplace coffee machine induction looks like this:

1. **Pair.** Assign a trained colleague as the new starter's guide.
2. **Show.** Walk through the checklist and the cheat sheet, including who to call.
3. **Do.** The new starter makes a drink while their guide watches.
4. **Confirm.** The guide signs off, and the new starter knows the golden rules: nobody touches the grinder, milk stays refrigerated and warnings get reported.

Aim to have at least two trained people at all times, so leave and resignations never leave you without a go-to person. If your trained pair both move on, ask your supplier for a refresher. That should be a normal request, not an awkward one.
